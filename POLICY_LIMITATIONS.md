# Policy Limitations and Residual Risk

## Purpose

The Phase B policy is designed to reduce risk, not to guarantee that synthetic media will never be deceptive, misused, misunderstood, or redistributed outside its intended context.

This section stress-tests the policy by identifying where it can fail even when the written rules appear strong.

## 1. Disclosure Can Be Removed

The policy requires redundant disclosure, but no disclosure method is permanent.

A viewer can crop a watermark, remove an opening title, extract a short clip, record a screen, add a misleading caption, or repost content on another platform.

The Task 6 workflow itself demonstrated how easy it is to create a derivative copy through screen recording. In that experiment, the HeyGen watermark remained visible, which was a positive outcome. The policy cannot assume that every later transformation will preserve it.

**Residual risk:** A responsibly created artifact may later circulate without the context that made it responsible.

**Compensating control:** For higher-risk content, prefer media that would not become seriously deceptive even if one disclosure layer disappeared.

## 2. Provenance Does Not Prove Truth

Provenance can show where a file came from or how it was handled. It does not prove that the script is true.

A synthetic video could have perfect internal records and still contain a false claim.

**Residual risk:** People may interpret "verified provenance" as "verified truth."

**Compensating control:** Keep factual verification separate from provenance requirements.

## 3. Detection Can Fail

The policy does not depend on automated deepfake detectors because detector performance can vary across compression levels, tools, and model generations.

Task 6 did not include an independent automated detector, so this project cannot claim firsthand evidence that a specific detector would reliably identify the HeyGen artifact.

**Residual risk:** A third party may encounter a synthetic copy with no watermark and no reliable detector result.

**Compensating control:** Prioritize creation-time controls—consent, truthfulness, disclosure, recordkeeping, and refusal—rather than relying on after-the-fact detection.

## 4. Human Reviewers Can Make Mistakes

Review adds accountability but does not guarantee correct decisions.

A reviewer may miss a false detail, misunderstand whether consent covers a reuse, treat a high-risk project as routine, approve inconsistent disclosure, or become overloaded by high-volume workflows.

**Residual risk:** A formally approved artifact may still be harmful.

**Compensating control:** Use elevated review for higher-risk categories, maintain records, and periodically audit decisions.

## 5. Consent Can Be Ambiguous

Even written consent can fail to capture every future context.

A person may agree to one synthetic announcement and later discover that the same model or recording can be used in ways they did not anticipate.

**Residual risk:** Technically valid consent may not feel meaningfully informed after technology or distribution patterns change.

**Compensating control:** Make consent purpose-specific, time-limited, revocable for future uses, and non-transferable to unrelated projects.

## 6. Bad-Faith Actors Can Ignore the Policy

A policy constrains authorized organizational behavior. It cannot physically prevent every employee, contractor, or outsider from violating the rules.

A determined bad-faith actor could use an external account, copy a voice sample, create content outside approved systems, remove disclosure, or publish anonymously.

**Residual risk:** Governance cannot eliminate intentional misconduct.

**Compensating control:** Access control, training, incident reporting, preserved logs, and organizational accountability reduce but do not remove this risk.

## 7. Platform Controls Are Not Portable

A platform may apply its own watermark, content label, or reporting mechanism. Those protections may disappear when content moves elsewhere.

**Residual risk:** The office loses control after publication.

**Compensating control:** Use platform controls as one layer rather than the only layer. Preserve internal evidence and build disclosures into the media itself.

## 8. Scale Can Outrun Review

The policy requires separate approval for automated or high-volume generation, but large workflows can still produce more outputs than humans can inspect individually.

**Residual risk:** A template error or bad data source can generate many incorrect outputs rapidly.

**Compensating control:** Use test cases, volume limits, sampling, monitoring, logs, and a shutdown mechanism.

## 9. Organizational Boundaries Are Imperfect

The policy is written for a University Athletics Communications Office. Other university units, external partners, athletes, fans, media outlets, and vendors may operate under different rules.

**Residual risk:** A synthetic artifact can cross organizational boundaries faster than governance can.

**Compensating control:** Contracts, vendor requirements, shared institutional standards, and public disclosure help, but cannot guarantee consistent behavior outside the office.

## 10. Technology Changes Faster Than Policy

Generation tools, cloning quality, watermark systems, content credentials, and detection techniques will continue to change.

A rule that is specific enough to work today can become outdated.

**Residual risk:** Staff may follow an old policy while using a capability the policy never anticipated.

**Compensating control:** Require periodic review and incident-triggered updates.

# Stress-Test Scenarios

## Scenario A: Well-Intentioned Shortcut

A staff member needs a schedule-update video quickly. They use a generic synthetic presenter but skip the disclosure because "everyone already knows this account uses AI sometimes."

The content is true, but the policy is violated. This reveals that convenience can erode disclosure even without malicious intent.

## Scenario B: Consent Reused Too Broadly

A coach consents to a synthetic voice for an accessibility project. Months later, a staff member reuses the voice for recruiting messages because the office still has the model.

The employee may believe they are acting efficiently, but the new use exceeds the original consent.

## Scenario C: Responsible Original, Misleading Repost

The office publishes a properly disclosed synthetic recap. A third party removes the label and posts an excerpt suggesting that the avatar is a real spokesperson.

The office followed the policy, but the downstream artifact is misleading.

## Scenario D: High-Volume Data Error

An automated personalization workflow pulls incorrect player data from a source system. Hundreds of synthetic videos are generated before a reviewer notices.

The policy can require controls, but it cannot guarantee that every data dependency is correct.

## Scenario E: Bad-Faith Internal Misuse

An employee deliberately uses an external account to create an unauthorized synthetic disciplinary statement about an athlete.

The policy cannot technically stop this act. Its role is to define that the behavior is prohibited, make monitoring and reporting possible, and provide a basis for response and accountability.

# Overall Limitation

The deepest limitation is that governance depends partly on people acting in good faith.

That is not unique to synthetic media. Policies governing privacy, security, finance, research, and communications all depend on some combination of human judgment, technical controls, oversight, and consequences.

The goal of this policy is therefore not perfect prevention. It is to make responsible behavior clear, make dangerous behavior harder to justify, create review points before publication, preserve evidence when something goes wrong, and ensure that the organization is willing to refuse uses whose risk exceeds their value.
